# Wildfire and smoke preparedness: sourced notes for First Nations communities

Compiled 14 November 2026. Every bullet cites a page that was fetched or returned by search that day. Items marked **[search only]** came from a search summary and were not confirmed by fetching the page itself; verify before quoting in the app.

Framing note: communities decide what fits their own governance, land and priorities. Indigenous fire stewardship (cultural burning, guardians) is knowledge held by Nations; this app should point to it and to Nation-led organisations, not describe or prescribe it.

---

## 1. Household actions

**Home ignition zone (FireSmart Canada).** Three zones within 30 m of the home.
- **Immediate zone, 0 to 1.5 m:** use non-combustible materials when building or renovating; clear vegetation and combustibles down to mineral soil and cover with gravel, brick or concrete; avoid woody shrubs and trees, or prune them regularly. [FireSmart Canada, Home Ignition Zone](https://firesmartcanada.ca/about.../the-home-ignition-zone/)
- **Intermediate zone, 1.5 to 10 m:** fire-resistant plants and non-combustible landscaping; no woody mulch; keep firewood, construction materials, furniture and tools out; move trailers, RVs and sheds to the extended zone, or put a gravel pad under and 1.5 m around them. [same page](https://firesmartcanada.ca/about.../the-home-ignition-zone/)
- **Extended zone, 10 to 30 m:** thin evergreens to at least 3 m between crowns; remove branches up to 2 m above ground; regularly clear fallen branches, dry grass and needles. [same page](https://firesmartcanada.ca/about.../the-home-ignition-zone/)
- The goal differs by zone: the immediate zone is about stopping embers collecting and igniting the house; the extended zone is about reducing fire intensity and rate of spread. The old 30 to 100 m "Zone 3" was dropped. **[search only]** [FireSmart BC, Home Ignition Zone](https://firesmartbc.ca/the-home-ignition-zone/)
- FireSmart Canada offers a home self-assessment PDF and the Begins at Home toolkit and app. [FireSmart Canada](https://firesmartcanada.ca/)

**Plan and go-bag (Government of Canada).**
- Three steps: know your local risks, make a household plan, build a kit. [Get Prepared](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/policing/emergencies/preparedness/get-prepared.html)
- Plan for everyone's needs (older adults, health needs, children, pets, service animals); practise the main escape route and alternatives; learn your local emergency organisation's evacuation procedures; keep the vehicle fuelled; check on elders and neighbours. [Wildfires: how to prepare](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/policing/emergencies/preparedness/get-prepared/hazards-emergencies/wildfires/how-prepare.html)
- Kit basics: about 4 L of water per person per day, non-perishable food, manual can opener, flashlight and radio with spare batteries, chargers, first aid kit (including face masks). Extras: cash, a couple of weeks of prescription medication, equipment for limited mobility, baby supplies, copies of key documents and your plan, and contact details. Keep it in a bag everyone knows how to find; replace perishables at least yearly. [Making an emergency kit](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/policing/emergencies/preparedness/get-prepared/emergency-kits.html)
- Smoke additions: a portable air cleaner, spare clean filters, and a well-fitted N95 or equivalent respirator. [Wildfires: how to prepare](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/policing/emergencies/preparedness/get-prepared/hazards-emergencies/wildfires/how-prepare.html)

**During a wildfire.**
- If a fire approaches call 9-1-1 or your local emergency number, follow official orders, and be ready to leave. Pack the kit in the vehicle, park facing out with windows closed, and leave a note of where you went if safe. Close doors and windows, cover vents, move propane BBQs, firewood and furniture away from the house, and shut off gas if evacuating. Use official evacuation routes only. [Wildfires: how to prepare](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/policing/emergencies/preparedness/get-prepared/hazards-emergencies/wildfires/how-prepare.html)
- Keep a sprinkler in good working order (the Government of Canada page says this; it does not give sprinkler design guidance). [same page](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/policing/emergencies/preparedness/get-prepared/hazards-emergencies/wildfires/how-prepare.html)

## 2. Community and band office actions

- **FireSmart Community Plan (also called Community Wildfire Protection or Resilience Plan):** a collaborative plan covering how wildfire could affect the community and practical local actions. Recommendations are organised under FireSmart disciplines: vegetation management, development, education, emergency planning, interagency cooperation, cross-training, legislation. Communities with a provincial or territorial program should first contact their FireSmart liaison. [FireSmart Canada, Community Planning](https://firesmartcanada.ca/community-planning/)
- **FireSmart Canada recognition programs:** Neighbourhood Recognition Program, Wildfire Community Preparedness Day, Advanced Home Assessment Program. The home page does not mention Indigenous-specific programs. [FireSmart Canada](https://firesmartcanada.ca/)
- **Indigenous Services Canada (ISC) Emergency Management Assistance Program (EMAP):** helps on-reserve communities prepare for, respond to and recover from emergencies. It funds First Nations-led non-structural mitigation and preparedness (training, emergency plan development and testing, risk assessments); structural mitigation is funded through other programs; response and recovery costs are reimbursed when needs exceed community capacity (wildfires included). [ISC, EMAP](https://www.sac-isc.gc.ca/eng/1534954090122)
- **ISC Emergency Management FireSmart funding stream** (separate from FireSmart Canada): for First Nations, tribal councils and Indigenous emergency management organisations. Eligible: firefighter training, wildfire risk assessments and review or update of response and evacuation plans, vegetation management, Indigenous knowledge activities (e.g. prescribed burns, fire history gathering), regional forums. Communities not previously funded get priority; BC communities apply through FNESS; others via the ISC regional emergency management contact. Equipment over $10,000 and capital items are ineligible. The page described the 2025-26 intake. [ISC FireSmart funding guidelines](https://www.sac-isc.gc.ca/eng/1743440237150)
- **Federal funding context (2026-27):** $18 million ongoing for First Nations preparedness and mitigation, $12.98 million for Emergency Management Coordinators, and $57.2 million over 2024-2029 for FireSmart. The page also says Chiefs and Councils lead response and recovery on reserve. [Public Safety Canada, 28 May 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-safety-canada/news/2026/05/the-government-of-canada-updates-on-the-2026-wildfire-season-preparedness-and-outlook.html)
- **Canadian Red Cross:** responds to First Nations evacuations at the request of community leadership under a standing agreement with ISC; the 2024/25 annual report says it supported 118 Indigenous communities and organisations. It has signed MOUs by invitation (e.g. Siksika Nation, 2021). No single standalone Red Cross First Nations wildfire preparedness program was found. **[search only]** [Red Cross, Resilience in Action](https://www.redcross.ca/inclusive-resilience/resilience-in-action), [Siksika MOU](https://www.redcross.ca/in-your-community/alberta/news-and-stories/siksika-nation-memorandum-of-understanding-collaborating-on-programs-and-services)
- **National Indigenous Fire Safety Council (NIFSC):** Indigenous-led national organisation, ISC-funded, focused on program and capacity supports, research, incident reporting, and fire prevention and public education. It leads a multi-year Wildfire Resilient Futures project under a Natural Resources Canada initiative, working with Indigenous communities on risk assessments and tools "designed with communities, not for them"; it invites communities to express interest in site visits. [UFV CHASI blog, Wildfire Resilient Futures](https://blogs.ufv.ca/chasi/?p=2193). Mandate summary **[search only]**: [NIFSC](https://indigenousfiresafety.azurewebsites.net/en/about/afac) (site did not load when fetched).
- **Communication:** Public health guidance recommends short, plain-language, tailored and translated messages sent more often; radio and door-to-door outreach may be needed for remote communities and older adults. [Wildfires in Canada: Toolkit for Public Health Authorities](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/publications/healthy-living/wildfires-canada-toolkit-public-health-authorities.html)
- **Cleaner air spaces:** the same toolkit calls them "an effective intervention to reduce population level exposure to wildfire smoke". Libraries, community centres and schools may look suitable but need steps to maintain indoor air quality; it recommends HVAC evaluations and refers to Health Canada's cleaner-air-spaces guidance and an FNHA "Wildfire Smoke and Clean Air Shelters" fact sheet. [Toolkit](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/publications/healthy-living/wildfires-canada-toolkit-public-health-authorities.html), [Health Canada, Guidance for Cleaner Air Spaces (PDF, not readable by fetch)](https://www.canada.ca/content/dam/hc-sc/documents/services/publications/healthy-living/guidance-cleaner-air-spaces-during-wildfire-smoke-events/guidance-cleaner-air-spaces-during-wildfire-smoke-events.pdf)
- **Coordination with fire agencies:** ISC has wildfire service agreements with six provinces (BC, AB, SK, MB, ON, QC) per the Auditor General. [OAG Report 8](https://www.canada.ca/en/auditor-general/our-work/audit-reports/parl-oag-202211-08-e.html) Researchers note wildfire and emergency management are provincial while First Nations are federal jurisdiction, an overarching factor in evacuations. **[search only]** [UBC Press, First Nations Wildfire Evacuations](https://ubcpress.ca/first-nations-wildfire-evacuations)
- **Gap:** no official page was fetched with specific structure-protection or sprinkler-system guidance for communities; point to a FireSmart liaison or provincial fire agency rather than stating technical specifications.

## 3. Wildfire smoke and health (Government of Canada)

**Who is most at risk.** People with lung or heart conditions, adults 65+, infants and young children, pregnant people; people working or exercising outdoors (including wildland firefighters); people in rural or remote areas; and people with lower income, housing insecurity or uncertain employment who may be less able to limit exposure. [Health Canada, Wildfire smoke and your health](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/healthy-living/wildfire-smoke-health.html)

**Symptoms.** Mild: headache, mild cough, eye, nose and throat irritation. Serious: dizziness, wheezing, chest pain, severe cough, asthma attack, shortness of breath, palpitations; talk to a provider or seek urgent care. [same page](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/healthy-living/wildfire-smoke-health.html)

**What to do by AQHI level** (as published on the ECCC AQHI page):
- Low 1-3: no restrictions.
- Moderate 4-6: general public carry on unless symptoms; at-risk people with symptoms consider cutting back hard exertion.
- High 7-10: general public with symptoms consider cutting back; at-risk people cut back or reschedule hard outdoor exertion.
- Very high 10+: general public cut back, especially with symptoms; at-risk people avoid strenuous outdoor activity.

Source: [ECCC, About the AQHI](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/air-quality-health-index/about.html). Most provinces and territories use AQHI+ in wildfire season (Quebec uses InfoSmog) **[search only]**: [Toolkit](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/publications/healthy-living/wildfires-canada-toolkit-public-health-authorities.html).

**Indoors.** Keep windows and doors closed; use a certified portable air cleaner that filters fine particles; use the best filter your ventilation system can handle and change it regularly (clogged filters do not work); limit exhaust fans. If it is too hot, cooling comes first: use air conditioning or go to a cooler cleaner-air space. Contact local authorities or your health authority for local cleaner-air spaces. [Health Canada, preparing for smoke events](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/health/healthy-living/environment/air-quality/wildfire-smoke/preparing-events.html), [Wildfire smoke and your health](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/healthy-living/wildfire-smoke-health.html)

**DIY box-fan filter (officially endorsed only as a short-term fallback).** Health Canada guidance says "There is some evidence that DIY air cleaners can be an effective option in short-term emergency situations, such as during a wildfire smoke event", and warns of limits and safety risks. Safety: clean box fan made 2012 or later with a safety fuse (ideally meeting UL 507); never leave it running unattended; keep clear of walls, furniture and curtains; no extension cord; no damaged fan; change filters regularly because clogged filters can overheat the fan. [Health Canada, indoor air quality in office buildings](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/healthy-living/guidance-improving-indoor-air-quality-office-buildings.html), [Toolkit](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/publications/healthy-living/wildfires-canada-toolkit-public-health-authorities.html). Build details (MERV 13 or higher filter, arrow direction) are from BCCDC, a provincial source, not Health Canada **[search only]**: [BCCDC fact sheet](https://www.bccdc.ca/resource-gallery/Documents/Guidelines%20and%20Forms/Guidelines%20and%20Manuals/Health-Environment/BCCDC_WildFire_FactSheet_BoxFanAirFilters.pdf).

**N95.** Health Canada recommends a well-fitting NIOSH-certified N95 or equivalent (KN95 or KF94) when you must be outdoors in smoke. [Health Canada, preparing for smoke events](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/health/healthy-living/environment/air-quality/wildfire-smoke/preparing-events.html)

**Also.** Keep a supply of medications, carry them in wildfire season, and talk to a health provider in advance about a plan. [same page](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/health/healthy-living/environment/air-quality/wildfire-smoke/preparing-events.html)

## 4. What communities and researchers say is needed

1. **Auditor General of Canada, Report 8, "Emergency Management in First Nations Communities, Indigenous Services Canada", tabled 15 November 2022.** Overall: ISC "did not provide the support First Nations communities needed to manage emergencies", and its work was reactive rather than preventative. [OAG Report 8](https://www.canada.ca/en/auditor-general/our-work/audit-reports/parl-oag-202211-08-e.html)
2. **Same report, key findings:** ISC had not resolved preparedness and mitigation problems first identified in 2013; it had not identified which communities were least able to manage emergencies; about $646 million (78%) of roughly $828 million spent from 2018-19 to 2021-22 went to response and recovery versus about $182 million (22%) to preparedness and mitigation; as of April 2022, 39% of eligible structural mitigation projects awaited funding (a backlog of 112). Of 1,352 emergencies from 2009-10 to 2021-22, 406 were wildfires. [OAG Report 8](https://www.canada.ca/en/auditor-general/our-work/audit-reports/parl-oag-202211-08-e.html)
3. **Same report, recommendations (3 highlighted on the page):** a risk-based approach to where to invest; address backlogs of unfunded and unreviewed structural mitigation projects; determine with First Nations how many emergency management coordinator positions are needed and fund them on risk and need. [OAG Report 8](https://www.canada.ca/en/auditor-general/our-work/audit-reports/parl-oag-202211-08-e.html) (ISC later committed to the seven recommendations in the full report **[search only]**: [QP note ISC-2023-QP-00729](https://search.open.canada.ca/qpnotes/record/isc-sac%2CISC-2023-QP-00729))
4. **Dr. Amy Cardinal Christianson** (Indigenous Leadership Initiative) told the House of Commons environment committee on 23 October 2024 that guardian programs should be expanded and funded so fire work (including cultural burning in spring and fall) is year-round and covers emergency management, planning, prevention, preparedness and recovery; that Nations are repeatedly excluded from fire management decisions; and that a distant federal force is not the answer. [ILI, Testimony on the Jasper National Park fires](https://www.ilinationhood.ca/publications/testimony-on-the-jasper-national-park-fires)
5. **McGee, Cardinal Christianson and the First Nations Wildfire Evacuation Partnership, *First Nations Wildfire Evacuations: A Guide for Communities and External Agencies* (UBC Press, 2021)** covers when to stage partial or full evacuation, transportation problems and proactive communication with the community. **[search only]**; chapter text not read. [UBC Press](https://ubcpress.ca/first-nations-wildfire-evacuations)
6. **NIFSC, Wildfire Resilient Futures:** says existing wildfire tools often do not reflect Indigenous realities, governance or cultural priorities, and is building community-driven risk assessment and decision-support tools. [UFV CHASI blog](https://blogs.ufv.ca/chasi/?p=2193)
7. **Government of Canada's own public health toolkit:** "Indigenous evacuees have faced inadequate language, cultural, health and spiritual supports." [Toolkit](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/publications/healthy-living/wildfires-canada-toolkit-public-health-authorities.html)
8. **Record seasons.** 2023: over 6,800 fires, over 14.6 million ha burned, over 230,000 people evacuated. [NRCan, Forest fires](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/forest-forestry/wildland-fires/forest-fires) (other counts of 15 to 18.5 million ha exist because of different methods and dates). 2025: 44,920 people from 61 on-reserve First Nations were displaced by wildfire; First Nations account for 42% of wildfire-related evacuations while being about 5% of the population. [Public Safety Canada, 28 May 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-safety-canada/news/2026/05/the-government-of-canada-updates-on-the-2026-wildfire-season-preparedness-and-outlook.html)

Not verified: a title of "Indigenous Fire Marshal" for any named person; the NIFSC founding year (sources conflict between 2020 and 2022).

## 5. Official Canadian fire danger rating

- **What it is.** The Fire Weather Index (FWI) is "a numeric rating of potential fire intensity used as an indicator of fire danger", based on noon weather; each provincial and territorial wildland fire agency calculates it at its weather stations. [NRCan, Fire weather](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/forest-forestry/wildland-fires/fire-weather)
- **Where to see it.** The Canadian Wildland Fire Information System (CWFIS) maps: [CWFIS FWI map](https://cwfis.cfs.nrcan.gc.ca/maps/fw?type=fwi) (URL given by NRCan; the CWFIS pages are script-driven and could not be read by fetch). Also link the local provincial or territorial agency, for example [Alberta fire danger](https://www.alberta.ca/fire-danger) or [BC fire danger](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/safety/wildfire-status/wildfire-situation/fire-danger) (BC page not fetched).
- **Class meanings (Alberta Wildfire wording; classes are agency-set, no national CWFIS definition page could be fetched):**
  - Low: fire can ignite but is not expected to spread to deeper vegetation or larger fuels such as trees.
  - Moderate: creeping or gentle surface fire is likely.
  - High: fuels are dry and fire risk is serious; moderate to vigorous surface fire, which may spread to treetops.
  - Very High: high-intensity fire expected, likely to spread to treetops; fire starts easily and challenges suppression.
  - Extreme: fuels extremely dry; fast-spreading, high-intensity fires are likely and are very difficult to control.

  Source: [Alberta Wildfire, Fire danger](https://www.alberta.ca/fire-danger)
- **Caution on numbers.** Class names and FWI cut-offs differ by jurisdiction. Example: the Northwest Territories is adding "Very High" to its older four-level signs, with FWI 0-4 Low, 5-10 Moderate, 11-20 High, 21-30 Very High, above 30 Extreme (NWT-specific) **[search only]**: [NWT, 2026 fire danger system updates](https://www.gov.nt.ca/ecc/en/services/wildfire-prevention-and-safety/2026-fire-danger-system-updates). A CWFIS metadata summary says national maps show classes as set by provincial and territorial agencies and may vary between provinces **[search only]**: [CWFIS metadata](https://cwfis.cfs.nrcan.gc.ca/datamart/metadata/fdr).
- **For the app.** Do not publish one national FWI-to-class table. Show our own estimate beside a link to the official map and the local agency's page, labelled "official rating set by your provincial or territorial fire agency."
